Overview

How animals are classified

The Animal Kingdom is organised into phyla using shared levels of body organisation, symmetry, germ layers and body cavities. Jump straight to any phylum below.

Porifera Cnidaria Ctenophora Platyhelminthes Nematoda Annelida Arthropoda Mollusca Echinodermata Hemichordata Chordata

Levels of organisation

From simple to complex: cellular → tissue → organ → organ-system.

Cellular – Porifera

Symmetry

Asymmetry (sponges), radial symmetry (jellies, adult echinoderms), bilateral symmetry (most phyla).

Radial & Bilateral

Diploblastic (two layers: Cnidaria, Ctenophora) vs triploblastic (three layers: all higher phyla).

Absent, pseudocoelom or true coelom — a key dividing feature between phyla.

In detail

Phylum by phylum

P

Porifera Cellular level

Sponges — the pore-bearing animals

  • Body with numerous pores (ostia) and a single opening, the osculum, connected by a water-canal system.
  • Most primitive phylum — organisation at the cellular level; no true tissues or organs.
  • Asymmetrical; mostly marine and sedentary (sessile) filter feeders.
  • Skeleton of calcareous or siliceous spicules, or fibres of spongin, or both.
  • Reproduce asexually by budding and gemmules, and sexually by gametes; internal fertilisation.
  • Regeneration capacity is exceptional.

Syconsycon sponge
Spongillafreshwater sponge
EuplectellaVenus' flower basket
Clionaboring sponge
C

Cnidaria Tissue level

Jellyfish, corals and sea anemones (Coelenterata)

  • Diploblastic with radial symmetry; well-developed tissue-level organisation.
  • Characteristic stinging cells called cnidocytes containing nematocysts, used for defence and prey capture.
  • Body with a blind gastrovascular cavity (single opening for intake and removal).
  • Two body forms — sessile polyp and free-swimming medusa; some (e.g. Obelia) show alternation of generations.
  • Nerve net for conduction; respiration and excretion by simple diffusion.
  • Mostly marine; Hydra is a freshwater example.

Representative species

C

Ctenophora Tissue level

Comb jellies or sea walnuts

  • Diploblastic, radially symmetrical, tissue-level bodies with collagenous mesoglea.
  • Identified by eight external rows of ciliated comb plates (ctenes) used for locomotion.
  • Exclusively marine, mostly free-swimming pelagic carnivores.
  • Many show bioluminescence (light emission from ctene rows).
  • Digestion intracellular and extracellular; some species lack nematocysts.
  • Division of labour is more advanced than in Cnidaria.

Representative species

Pleurobrachiasea gooseberry
Ctenoplanaflat-bodied comb jelly
Beroenematocladous comb jelly
P

Platyhelminthes Organ level

Flatworms

  • Triploblastic, bilaterally symmetrical and acoelomate — flattened dorso-ventrally.
  • First phyla with organ-level organisation; body soft, unsegmented (some segmented in tapeworms).
  • Digestive system incomplete or absent; no respiratory or circulatory organs — gas exchange by diffusion.
  • Excretion and osmoregulation by flame cells (protonephridia).
  • Free-living forms (planarians) and many endoparasites (flukes, tapeworms) with suckers and hooks.
  • Mostly hermaphrodites; free-living forms show great regenerative ability.

Representative species

Planariaplanarian / Dugesia

N

Nematoda Pseudocoelom

Roundworms (Aschelminthes)

  • Triploblastic, bilaterally symmetrical and pseudocoelomate.
  • Cylindrical, unsegmented body, circular in cross-section, covered by a tough cuticle.
  • Organ-system level of organisation with a complete digestive tract (mouth and anus).
  • Excretory system simple — when present, renette cells or canals.
  • Often parasitic (many cause disease); sexes separate (dioecious) with sexual dimorphism.
  • Widely distributed in soil, water and as parasites of plants and animals.

Representative species

Ascarisintestinal roundworm
Wuchereriafilarial worm
Ancylostomahookworm
Caenorhabditis elegansmodel organism
A

Annelida True coelom

Segmented worms

  • Triploblastic, bilaterally symmetrical, coelomate and metamerically segmented.
  • Organ-system level organisation; long, segmented, usually cylindrical bodies.
  • Closed circulatory system with haemoglobin-like blood pigment; respiration through skin or gills.
  • Excretion by nephridia; locomotion aided by chaetae (setae).
  • Found in marine, freshwater and terrestrial habitats.
  • Earthworms and leeches bear a special clitellum involved in cocoon formation.

Representative species

Pheretimaearthworm
Hirudinariamedicinal leech
Nereisragworm / clam worm
A

Arthropoda Largest phylum

Insects, spiders, crabs, centipedes — the jointed-legged animals

  • The largest phylum — roughly two-thirds of all known animal species.
  • Triploblastic, bilaterally symmetrical, coelomate; organ-system organisation.
  • Segmented body with jointed appendages and a chitinous exoskeleton that is periodically moulted.
  • Open circulatory system (haemocoel); respiration by gills, tracheae or book lungs.
  • Excretion by Malpighian tubules (insects) or green/antennal glands (crustaceans).
  • Highly diverse sensory organs; many show metamorphosis; sexes usually separate.

M

Mollusca Second largest

Snails, clams, squid and octopus — the soft-bodied animals

  • Second largest phylum; triploblastic, bilaterally symmetrical, coelomate.
  • Body typical of three parts — head-foot, visceral mass and a dorsal mantle.
  • Mantle usually secretes a calcareous shell (reduced or absent in squid and octopus).
  • Open circulatory system (haemocoel); gills or lungs lie in the mantle cavity.
  • Feeding by a rasping tongue-like radula (absent in bivalves); excretion by nephridia.
  • Mostly oviparous; enormous habitat range from seas to land and freshwater.

E

Echinodermata Exclusively marine

Starfish, sea urchins and sea cucumbers — the spiny-skinned animals

  • Exclusively marine, triploblastic, coelomate with organ-system organisation.
  • Adults show radial (five-rayed, pentamerous) symmetry; larvae are bilaterally symmetrical.
  • Calcareous endoskeletal plates (ossicles) often with spines projecting through the skin.
  • Unique water vascular system with tube feet for locomotion, feeding and gas exchange.
  • No heart and no distinct blood vessels; circulate via the water vascular and coelomic fluid.
  • Usually dioecious with external fertilisation; some show remarkable regeneration.

Representative species

Asteriasstarfish

Antedonsea lily / feather star

Holothuriasea cucumber
H

Hemichordata Half-chordates

Acorn worms

  • Marine, worm-like, triploblastic, bilaterally symmetrical and coelomate.
  • Body divided into three regions — proboscis, collar and trunk.
  • Posterior gill slits (pharyngeal clefts) present; a dorsal hollow nerve cord in the collar.
  • Possess a stomochord (buccal diverticulum) in the collar — a "false notochord".
  • Development sometimes shows a tornaria larva resembling echinoderm larvae.
  • Considered a connecting link between non-chordates and chordates.

Representative species

Balanoglossusacorn worm
Saccoglossusacorn worm
C

Chordata Most advanced

Vertebrates and their closest relatives

  • Define the phylum: a notochord, dorsal hollow nerve cord, and pharyngeal gill slits at some stage, plus a post-anal tail.
  • Triploblastic, bilaterally symmetrical and coelomate with the highest organ-system organisation.
  • Closed circulatory system with a ventral heart; kidneys for excretion.
  • Subphylum Urochordata (Tunicata): Ascidia, Salpa, Herdmania — sea squirts.
  • Subphylum Cephalochordata: Branchiostoma (Amphioxus or lancelet).
  • Subphylum Vertebrata includes Agnatha (Petromyzon, Myxine) and Gnathostomata — fishes (Scoliodon, Labeo), Rana (amphibians), reptiles, Aves and Mammalia.

Study tip: remember the order

Memorise the phyla in sequence: Porifera → Cnidaria → Ctenophora → Platyhelminthes → Nematoda → Annelida → Arthropoda → Mollusca → Echinodermata → Hemichordata → Chordata — roughly from simplest body plan to most advanced.
